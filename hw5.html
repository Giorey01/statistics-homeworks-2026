Homework 5 · Welford's recurrences and sampling distributions

Sampling mean and variance of penetration scores

1. Welford's recurrence formulas

In intrusion detection systems and continuous monitoring, observations arrive sequentially: x1, x2, …. Storing past data for multi-pass calculations is impractical; running estimates of mean and variance update in O(1) time and space per observation.

For a stream of k values, we define:

Mk = (1/k) Σi≤k xi   (sample mean after k observations)
Sk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)²   (sum of squared deviations from Mk)
s²k = Sk/(k − 1)  (unbiased sample variance, k ≥ 2),    Vk = Sk/k  (population variance)

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Isolating the latest arrival xk from the accumulated sum:

k·Mk = x1 + … + xk−1 + xk = (k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk
⇒ Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)/k    (with initial conditions M0 = 0, M1 = x1)

The updated mean adjusts the previous estimate by a fraction 1/k of the deviation xk − Mk−1. Rearranging this identity gives two expressions used in the variance derivation: k·(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1 and (k − 1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk.

Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

Expressing Sk through algebraic expansion gives Sk = Σi≤k xi² − k·Mk². The difference between successive steps k − 1 and k simplifies as:

Sk − Sk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k − 1)·Mk−1² (the sum of squares of the first k − 1 terms cancels out)
    = xk² − k·Mk² + (k·Mk − xk)·Mk−1 (substituting (k − 1)Mk−1 = kMk − xk)
    = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − Mk·k(Mk − Mk−1) (factoring by xk and Mk)
    = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − Mk·(xk − Mk−1) (applying Knuth: k(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1)
⇒ Sk = Sk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk)    (S0 = S1 = 0)

The update step is the product of the distance of xk from the previous mean and from the new mean. Because xk − Mk = ((k − 1)/k)(xk − Mk−1), this step also equals ((k − 1)/k)(xk − Mk−1)². Both factors share the same sign, so the increment is never negative and Sk never decreases.

Direct recurrence for the variance

Dividing Sk by k or k − 1 allows updating the variance directly, mirroring the structure of Knuth's mean formula:

Vk = Vk−1 + (d·e − Vk−1) / k   (population variance)
s²k = s²k−1 + (d·e − s²k−1) / (k − 1)   (sample variance, k ≥ 2)

where d = xk − Mk−1 and e = xk − Mk. The mean is a running average of incoming values; the variance is a running average of the cross-deviation products d·e.

Implementation

const w = { k: 0, mean: 0, ss: 0 };

function welfordPush(w, x) {
  w.k += 1;
  const d = x - w.mean;
  w.mean += d / w.k;
  w.ss += d * (x - w.mean);
}

const sampleVariance = (w) => (w.k > 1 ? w.ss / (w.k - 1) : NaN);

The variable d uses the old mean before updating w.mean, while (x - w.mean) uses the updated mean.

Step by step

Numerical stability vs. textbook formula

The standard algebraic formula s² = (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n − 1) is exact on paper, but unstable under floating-point arithmetic. When data values are large relative to their variance, subtracting two large and nearly identical quantities cancels out leading digits, losing precision to catastrophic cancellation. Welford's algorithm accumulates deviations from the running mean at every step, keeping intermediate terms scaled to the variance.

When shifting the sample by c = 109, individual squared terms exceed 1018, beyond the exact representation limit of 53-bit IEEE 754 doubles (≈ 9·1015). In this regime, the naive formula produces a negative variance. A two-pass algorithm avoids this issue by computing the mean first, but requires storing all observations. Welford achieves identical numerical stability in a single streaming pass with O(1) memory overhead.

4. Ensemble averages of sample statistics

For a given sample size n, each of the m attackers computes x̄j and s²j from their own n attempts. Averaging these quantities across the entire ensemble verifies their theoretical expectations: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ² hold for any sample size n ≥ 2, establishing both as unbiased estimators. In contrast, normalizing the sum of squares by n yields E[S/n] = ((n − 1)/n)·σ², which underestimates the variance by Bessel's correction factor.

5. Sampling variability and sample size

While ensemble averages align with the true parameters, estimates from any single attacker disperse around μ and σ². For i.i.d. observations, the theoretical sampling variances are:

Var(x̄) = σ²/n    (standard error: σ/√n)
Var(s²) = (μ₄ − σ⁴·(n − 3)/(n − 1))/n = σ⁴·(2/(n − 1) + κ/n)

6. Inferential confidence from a single sample

In real settings, μ and σ² are unknown and must be estimated from a single sample of size n. With estimated standard error s/√n, nominal 95% confidence intervals are x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n for normal approximations, or x̄ ± t0.975, n−1·s/√n for small samples. Running this across m attackers tests how often these intervals actually contain μ.

7. Conclusions

Empirical results

Across all tested sample sizes, the ensemble average of sample means converges to μ = 3 within simulation error (|z| ≤ 2). This unbiasedness depends solely on the linearity of expectation, without requiring asymptotic sample sizes or normality.

The average sample variance matches σ² = 2.1 when divided by n − 1. Dividing by n underestimates variance by a factor of (n − 1)/n: a 50% deficit at n = 2, 10% at n = 10, and negligible differences past n = 100.

Sampling distributions and convergence

An unbiased estimator can still be noisy for a single attacker. Larger n keeps the centers at μ = 3 and σ² = 2.1 while shrinking estimator variance: Var(x̄) scales as σ²/n, and Var(s²) scales asymptotically as 1/n.

Estimating variance requires more data than estimating the mean because Var(s²) depends on the fourth moment μ₄. For Binomial(10, 0.3), excess kurtosis is small (κ ≈ −0.124), so estimates do not suffer from heavy-tail spikes, though distributions at n = 2 and n = 5 remain right-skewed.

For confidence intervals, replacing the Gaussian critical value 1.96 with Student's t quantiles widens intervals at small n to account for variability in s. As sample size grows, coverage approaches the nominal 95% rate in accordance with the central limit theorem.

Application to cybersecurity metrics

Ranking penetration testers or defenses on small samples (such as n = 5) mostly measures luck. Telling skill levels apart requires an n large enough that the standard error s/√n is smaller than the gap between testers.

In monitoring pipelines, Welford's recurrence updates baselines and alert thresholds in O(1) space per stream without retaining raw logs.

Model assumptions

These formulas assume independent, identically distributed trials. If attempts correlate through attacker learning, automated scripts, or shared credentials, Var(x̄) exceeds σ²/n and s/√n underestimates uncertainty. If target systems vary in difficulty, μ and σ² describe the mixed pool of systems, not any single host.
