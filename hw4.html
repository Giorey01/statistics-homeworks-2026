Modeling a penetration process

1. The model

An attacker tries to break n systems, one after the other. Step i is the attack on system i and gives a score Xi; the score after k systems is Sk = X1 + … + Xk, so the sequence S1, S2. They are i.i.d.: independent (the outcome on one system does not affect the next) and identically distributed (all the systems are equally hard to hack). The m attackers are independent copies: each one produces one trajectory. So n is the length of a trajectory and m is the number of trajectories, so in the Monte Carlo repetitions n changes what we look at, m only changes how precisely we see it.

3. The three quantities

From the same final scores of the m attackers we build three numbers, which differ only in what Sn is divided by. With Xi i.i.d. of mean μ and variance σ², E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ² (variances add up because the steps are independent), and Var(c·Y) = c²·Var(Y). Dividing by n is too much, not dividing is too little, √n is the scale that keeps the variance fixed.

4. Law of Large Numbers

Sn/n is the penetration rate of one attacker. The Law of Large Numbers says that it converges in probability to μ: for every margin ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞. The table uses the simulation of Part 3 (same model, p and m) and estimates the probability as the fraction of attackers farther than ε from μ.

6. Central Limit Theorem

The LLN says where Sn/n goes.
The Central Limit Theorem says what the fluctuations around it look like at the scale √n: (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to N(0, 1), whatever the law of the single Xi. In distribution means that the CDF tends to Φ at every point: the shape converges, not the values. In the Rademacher case μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the standardized sum is exactly Sn/√n. Two measures: the histogram as a density against the bell curve φ(z) = e−z²/2/√(2π), and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance D = maxz |Fm(z) − Φ(z)|, the largest gap between the empirical CDF of the m attackers and Φ.

7. How many attackers?

The attackers are independent, so m does not change the law of the score of one attacker. It changes two other things: how precisely the simulation sees that law (the errors of the estimates shrink like 1/√m), and the worst case: the more attackers, the more likely that at least one of them has an extreme score.

7a. Precision of the estimates

Rademacher, n = 100, increasing m. In grey the 95% band around the true value: an estimate from m attackers falls inside it 95% of the time, and the band shrinks like 1/√m.

7b. The luckiest attacker

Bernoulli: m attackers against n systems, and for each m the highest number of breached systems. The threshold is np + 2σ√n: by the CLT a single attacker exceeds it with probability q = 1 − Φ(2) ≈ 2.3%, so at least one of m attackers exceeds it with probability 1 − (1 − q)m.

8. Interpretation

Systems and attackers

  • The total score grows with the size of the system, its relative uncertainty shrinks. E[Sn] = nμ grows like n, the spread σ√n only like √n. With p = 0.3 and n = 1000 an attacker breaks 300 ± 14.5 systems: more damage in absolute terms, but more predictable in proportion.
  • The penetration rate Sn/n settles on p (LLN). On many systems one attacker is enough to estimate how good they are. On few systems it is not: with n = 10 an average attacker (p = 0.3) has standard deviation 0.145 on the rate and can look harmless or dangerous by pure chance. Rating an attacker, or a defence, on a handful of systems gives unreliable numbers.
  • The fluctuations are Gaussian at the scale √n (CLT). So they can be bounded in advance: more than np + 2σ√n breaches happens to about 2.3% of the attackers (with n = 1000 and p = 0.3, more than 329). This is what lets a defender size alarms and margins.
  • Zero mean is not zero risk (Rademacher). With attack and defence even, nobody wins on average, but a single attacker drifts about √n levels away from 0: with n = 10000 about 5% of them end beyond ±196 levels.
  • The number of attackers m does not change the law of one attacker. It makes the estimates sharper (errors like 1/√m) and, above all, it raises the worst case: with q = 2.3% per attacker, 100 attackers give a 90% chance that at least one crosses the threshold, and the highest score grows like σ√n·√(2 ln m). A defender faces the luckiest attacker, not the average one.
  • The aggregate score of all attackers is a sum of n·m i.i.d. steps: mean nmμ, variance nmσ². The same LLN and CLT apply with n·m in place of n, so the overall breach rate is even more concentrated around p (standard deviation √(p(1−p)/(nm))).

Limits of the model

  • Systems are not identical. Each system has its own pi: the steps are independent but not identically distributed. LLN and CLT still hold under mild conditions (Lindeberg), with mean Σpi and variance Σpi(1 − pi).
  • Steps are not independent. An attacker who breaks a system reuses what they learned (credentials, lateral movement): positive correlation makes Var(Sn) grow faster than nσ², and the σ√n margins become too optimistic.
  • Attackers are not independent. They share exploits or coordinate, which makes extreme outcomes more likely than 1 − (1 − q)m.
  • The score is a bare count. No severity, no time, no absorbing states (a detected attacker usually stops). The symmetric Rademacher walk is an idealization used to isolate the zero-mean case.
