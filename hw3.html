Encrypting with the curve, and breaking it

1. Encrypting with the elliptic curve

Bob’s public key Q = kG tells everyone who he is, but it doesn’t send him any secret on its own. ElGamal is the classic way to do that:

  • Alice picks a random number r, used only once.
  • She computes a shared secret S = rQ and uses it to hide the message.
  • She sends Bob the hint R = rG together with the hidden message.
  • Bob computes S = kR and uncovers the message.

Bob gets the same S because of one line:

S = r·Q = r·(k·G) = k·(r·G) = k·R

Alice knows r and Bob knows k. They reach the same point from opposite sides. An eavesdropper sees only Q and R, and getting S from those means solving the elliptic-curve discrete log problem, which is believed to be infeasible.

Other ways to encrypt a text taking advantage of ECC: ECIES (Elliptic Curve Integrated Encryption Scheme), HPKE (Hybrid Public Key Encryption, RFC 9180), ECDH + AES-GCM, Menezes–Vanstone, Identity-Based Encryption (Boneh–Franklin)

Alice knows r and Q, Bob knows k and R. A man in the middle could see Q and R and would need k or r: that is the discrete-logarithm problem of Homework 1.

ElGamal is not the only way. In practice the shared secret of ECDH feeds a symmetric cipher (TLS 1.3, SSH, Signal), ECIES and its modern successor HPKE (RFC 9180) encrypt to a public key, and Massey–Omura and Menezes–Vanstone are older variants that are no longer used. Part 1b below is a simplified ECIES.

1a. By hand on the toy curve (p = 17)

On y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17) with G = (1, 5) every point is a multiple of G, and G has order 9. In this version the message itself must be a point, M = mG. Since aG + bG = (a + b)G, every step can be checked by adding the numbers in front of G modulo 9.

Same k and M, every possible r

1b. On the real secp256k1, with a small key

Here the curve is the real secp256k1 (the parameters of Ethereum), but the private key is restricted to 1 ≤ k ≤ 2²⁰, about a million values, so that Part 5 can attack it. A text is not a point, so, as in ECIES, the shared secret S becomes the key of a symmetric cipher: the message bytes are XORed with the 32 bytes of S.x. The message can therefore be at most 32 bytes long.

This is a toy. The private key has only 20 bits, the mask is S.x used directly instead of passing it through a key-derivation function, the message is limited to 32 bytes, and nothing protects the ciphertext from being modified. Part 5 shows how the small key is broken.

2. The Caesar cipher

Each letter is shifted forward by k positions in the alphabet (with wrap-around: Z + 1 = A). Decryption shifts back by k. The same key is used for both, so it is a symmetric cipher.

3. Comparing the two schemes

Both schemes take a message and a secret and produce a ciphertext, but they differ in who holds the key, in how many keys exist, and in what the ciphertext reveals about the message.

What they have in common

  • The algorithm is public and only the key is secret (Kerckhoffs's principle).
  • Both are modular arithmetic: mod 26 for Caesar, mod p for the curve.
  • Both hide the message with an invertible mask: Caesar adds k to every letter, the ECC XORs every byte with S.x.
    The difference is the mask: Caesar uses the same value k at every position, the ECC a sequence of bytes that looks random. That is where the statistical difference below comes from.
  • In both toys the weakness is a key space small enough to try every key.

4. Statistical attack on Caesar

The Caesar ciphertext keeps the letter frequencies of the message, shifted by k. So the attacker decrypts with all 26 keys and keeps the attempt that looks most like English. To measure this we need the letter frequencies of the language, estimated from a long text (called a corpus).

4a. The corpus

Letter frequencies of English, estimated on the first half (dashed line = 1/26)

4b. Breaking one ciphertext

For every key the decrypted text gets a score. χ² compares its letter counts Oi with the counts expected in English Ei = N·fi: χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² / Ei, smaller is better. Here χ² is a score to rank 26 candidates, not a hypothesis test: with a few letters Ei is far below 5 and the χ² distribution does not apply. The bigram score also uses the order of the letters: it adds the log-probabilities of every pair of consecutive letters (TH, HE, IN, …), larger is better.

4c. How many letters are needed?

Monte Carlo simulation: for each length L, take a random piece of L letters from the second half of the corpus (never used to estimate the frequencies), encrypt it with a random key, attack it, and count a success when the decrypted text equals the original. Three methods: χ², bigrams, and the naive "the most frequent letter is E".

Results. With 1 letter χ² always answers "E", so it succeeds about 12% of the time: the frequency of E. χ² passes 90% at about 15–20 letters and is almost always right from 30: the longer the text, the closer its frequencies get to the true ones. "Most frequent = E" throws away 25 letters out of 26 and needs hundreds of letters; bigrams are right about 97% of the time already with 10. The ± is the 95% interval 1.96·√(p(1 − p)/T).

Limits. Short texts, a different language from the corpus (Italian has few H and many A, I, O), unusual texts (lists of names, a lipogram without E), and the attacker must know it is Caesar: a general substitution or Vigenère needs other methods, still statistical.

5. Brute-forcing the small ECC key

The attacker sees Bob's public key Q and the intercepted ciphertext (R, c) of Part 1b, and knows (Kerckhoffs) that 1 ≤ k ≤ 220. They try every key: 1·G = Q? 2·G = Q? … Instead of a full multiplication per try, each candidate is the previous one plus G: (i + 1)G = iG + G, one point addition per key. Once k is found, they decrypt exactly like Bob: S = kR, then XOR.

Why it fails on a real key

Every extra bit doubles the work. The times below use the speed measured on this computer by the attack above (worst case: all the keys).

On secp256k1 the key is uniform in [1, n − 1] with n ≈ 2256. The best known attack is not brute force but Pollard's rho, about √n ≈ 2128 point additions: this is why the curve is said to give 128-bit security. The exception is a large quantum computer, where Shor's algorithm solves the ECDLP in polynomial time.
