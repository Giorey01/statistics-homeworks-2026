Homework 1 · Public-key cryptography

Private key → public key in Ethereum

A toy version of what an Ethereum wallet does with secp256k1: multiply a base point G by a secret number k and publish the result Q = kG. Going forward is easy; going back from Q to k is the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP). This page computes it step by step, animates it, and then studies the statistical behaviour of the map k → kG.

1. The idea in one minute

The curve is y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17), the same equation as secp256k1 but with a tiny prime instead of a 256-bit one. Its points, plus a special "point at infinity" O, form a group: two points can be added and the result is again a point of the curve.

Scalar multiplication is repeated addition: kG = G + G + … + G (k times). The private key is the number k, the public key is the point Q = kG.

Here: base point G = (1, 5), private key k = 6.

Adding two points P + Q = R

slope λ = (yQ − yP) / (xQ − xP)   if P ≠ Q
slope λ = 3xP² / 2yP   if P = Q (doubling)
xR = λ² − xP − xQ
yR = λ(xP − xR) − yP

Everything is modulo 17. "Dividing" means multiplying by the modular inverse. If Q = −P (same x, opposite y) the result is O.

Step by step: repeated addition

The fast method: double-and-add

Real keys are 256-bit numbers, so adding G one time after another is impossible. Double-and-add reads the bits of k from the right: at each bit the helper point A is doubled, and it is added to the result R only when the bit is 1. It needs about log₂ k steps instead of k.

What to notice. The jumps look erratic: there is no visible pattern linking k to the position of kG. But after 9 steps the walk reaches O and starts again: G = (1, 5) has order 9, so on this curve only 8 different public keys exist and k and k + 9 give the same one. The curve has 18 points in total, but this base point reaches only half of them. On secp256k1 the order is a prime of about 2256, so every key gives a different point.

4. Statistical exploration

The experiment is repeated for every private key k = 1 … n − 1 (n = order of the base point), and the resulting points are studied as data. With p = 17 there are at most 17 outputs, too few for any test, so the same curve equation is also used with the prime p = 8191 (8011 points, a prime number, as on secp256k1).

4.1 Distribution of the x and y coordinates

Histogram of x

Histogram of y

On the large curve both histograms are flat and the means are close to (p − 1)/2. But the flatness holds only at coarse grain: only about half of the possible x values ever appear (those for which x³ + 7 is a square), and each of them appears exactly twice, with opposite y. The outputs are uniform over the points of the curve, not over all the numbers from 0 to p − 1.

4.2 Frequency of individual values

Every point is produced exactly once: k → kG is a one-to-one map (a permutation of the group), so there are no collisions and no missing outputs. A random function behaves differently: throwing N balls into N boxes leaves about 1/e ≈ 37% of the boxes empty. This single number already separates kG from a hash.

4.4 Apparent uniformity (χ² test)

The test uses a random sample of 1000 keys and 10 equal bins; with 9 degrees of freedom uniformity is rejected at the 5% level when χ² > 16.92. The numbers change at every run because the sample is random. "Not rejected" does not mean "proved uniform": from 4.1 we know that half of the x values never occur, the 10-bin test is simply too coarse to see it.

4.5 Correlation between input and output

k (horizontal) against x(kG) (vertical); dashed line at k = n/2

Pearson's r is practically zero: knowing k does not help to predict the coordinates with a straight line. But the scatter plot is a perfect mirror image around k = n/2, because (n − k)G = −(kG) has the same x. And (k + 1)G = kG + G, so each output is computed from the previous one with a public formula. Zero correlation is not independence: the relation is exact, it is just not linear.

4.6 Avalanche-like behaviour

Mean fraction of output bits changed, per flipped bit of k

One bit of k is flipped and the bits of the new point (x followed by y) are compared with the old ones. On the large curve about half of the output bits change, as for a hash. But the two outputs are not unrelated: flipping bit i gives k′G = kG ± 2iG, the old point plus or minus a fixed, public point. Anyone can compute one from the other without knowing k. For a hash function such a relation would be a break.

4.7 Different base points

On the toy curve the base point decides everything, because the group order 18 is not prime:

On the large curve the order 8011 is prime, so every point generates the whole group: changing the base point only changes the order in which the same points are visited, and the histograms and bit frequencies stay identical (try it with the selector above). This is why real curves are chosen with a prime order.

5. Discussion: one-way function or hash function?

k → kG and a hash such as Keccak-256 are both deterministic, easy to compute and hard to invert. The similarity ends there. A one-way function is only required to be hard to invert. A cryptographic hash must also accept inputs of any length, make collisions impossible to find and show no relation at all between the outputs of related inputs.

Meaningful for a one-way function (kG)

  • Hardness of inversion (ECDLP): the defining property. It is computational, not statistical: none of the six experiments can measure it.
  • Uniformity over the group: a uniform key gives a uniform public key.
  • Injectivity: no two keys share a public key.
  • Large prime order: no small subgroups (the toy curve shows what happens otherwise).

Specific to cryptographic hash functions

  • Compression of inputs of arbitrary length to a fixed size.
  • Collision resistance: collisions exist but cannot be found. For kG they do not exist at all.
  • Strict avalanche: outputs of related inputs are unrelated.
  • Random-oracle behaviour: unbiased, independent output bits, no algebraic structure.

The structure is a feature. The rule (k₁ + k₂)G = k₁G + k₂G makes kG a bad hash, and it is exactly what digital signatures (ECDSA), key agreement (Diffie–Hellman) and wallet key derivation (BIP-32) are built on. This is also why Ethereum never uses a public key directly as an identifier: it passes it through Keccak-256 first (see Homework 2).

The limit of statistics. On the toy curve the statistics have the same shape as on the large one, yet inverting it takes at most 8 attempts. Statistical tests can refute randomness; they can never prove security. "Looks random" is measured, "is hard to invert" is conjectured.

6. Research: cryptography and statistics in the most famous blockchains

A blockchain is a ledger replicated among computers that do not trust each other. Cryptography guarantees who can write (signatures) and that the past cannot be changed (hashes). Statistics guarantees that the network agrees: security is probabilistic, an attack is not impossible but its probability is made negligible.

Bitcoin: mining as a random experiment

  • Each hash attempt is a Bernoulli trial with a tiny success probability: the number of attempts is geometric, the time between blocks is approximately exponential (mean 10 minutes) and the blocks per hour are Poisson (mean 6).
  • The difficulty adjustment every 2016 blocks is an estimate of the network's computing power from a sample of waiting times.
  • The double-spend probability in the Bitcoin whitepaper is a random-walk (gambler's ruin) calculation: with 10% of the power and 6 confirmations it is about 0.02%, which is the origin of the "wait 6 blocks" rule.

Ethereum: consensus by random sampling

  • Every 12 seconds one validator is drawn at random, with probability proportional to its stake, to propose a block.
  • Validators are shuffled into committees: a committee is a random sample of the population, sized so that a dishonest majority has a negligible (binomial-tail) probability.
  • BLS signatures can be aggregated thanks to the same kind of algebraic structure studied above.
